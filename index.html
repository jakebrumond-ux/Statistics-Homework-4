Statistics

Reading the page. Var(Sₙ)=nσ² grows, Var(Sₙ/n)=σ²/n shrinks to 0 (LLN: the fraction of attackers with |Sₙ/n−μ|>ε goes to 0), and Var(Sₙ/√n)=σ² stays constant. In the Rademacher case Sₙ/√n approaches N(0,1) (CLT); in the Bernoulli case it drifts with mean √n·p, so the standardized version (Sₙ−np)/√(np(1−p)) is what converges. Larger m only sharpens the empirical picture; it does not change a single attacker’s distribution.

Interpretation: n systems and m attackers

  • Increasing n: adds more score increments to each trajectory. The total’s typical fluctuation scale is √n, while the average becomes more stable and concentrates near μ.
  • Increasing m: does not change the theoretical distribution; it increases the number of independent paths and generally improves the accuracy of estimated histograms and sample statistics.
  • Rademacher model: positive and negative increments cancel on average, producing a zero long-run mean and a normalized total with an approximately normal shape for large n.
  • Bernoulli model: increments are nonnegative, so the average estimates the success probability p. The normalized total needs centering to apply the CLT when p is nonzero.
Conclusion: n controls the behavior of each accumulated process, while m controls the precision of the simulation. The LLN explains concentration of averages; the CLT explains the approximately Gaussian shape of properly centered and scaled sums.

Suggested experiment report

  1. Run both models for n = 10, 100, 1000 while keeping m fixed.
  2. Record empirical means and variances of Sₙ, Sₙ/n and Sₙ/√n (see the statistics table above).
  3. For Rademacher, observe the normalized histogram approach the standard normal curve as n increases.
  4. Repeat with larger m and explain how histograms become less noisy without changing the theoretical distribution.
